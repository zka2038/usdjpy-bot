Measured against academic FX microstructure, anomaly cost research, pre-registration practice and backtesting protocols, spec 004's gap-fill and cost plan is **right about how to charge a spread and wrong in four places about how to aggregate, impute and judge it**. Two choices match field practice almost exactly. The first is charging each paying leg its own instant ask−bid (Q1), which is what the closest published Tokyo-fix backtest does. The second is keeping uniform assumption columns beside a best-available governing column (Q3). Step 0 validation (Q5) and date-keyed era multipliers (Q6) are acceptable as declared, provided the era columns stay stress columns and never govern. Four choices depart from the field. Dropping no-quote trades from the governing measured column (Q2) is complete-case analysis, which the anomaly-cost literature rejects explicitly. Charging a pooled yearly **median** for 2015–2024 (Q4) understates expected cost under right-skewed spreads; every aggregate found in the literature is a mean, and per-trade quotes take precedence wherever they exist. The verdict mechanics of criterion 5 (Q7) add three problems: a point-estimate margin, a 1-SE "not resolved" band that is looser than any 95% convention, and a bp view at 159 that the literature does not use. The fetch rule (Q8) has a sound stopping rule but no declared fallback. The most consequential finding concerns the as-run prefix margin: **+0.850 pips (SE 0.663) is labelled "pass", but its one-sided 95% lower bound is −0.240, so under standard confidence-interval logic it is not resolved**. In the field's own test, net t under pessimistic costs, the effect clears the repo's 2.5 bar by only about 0.24 pips of cost headroom on the prefix and 0.03 on the postfix, and that headroom rests entirely on the 0.360 / 0.272-pip synthetic spreads the measured column will replace. None of these fixes requires touching a threshold. Three are dated amendments that must be made before filled data is viewed. The rest are reported-only diagnostics.

A note on figures. Several research notes, and CLAUDE.md, quote pre-fix numbers: prefix +2.068 pips, Sharpe 0.743, SE ≈ 0.657, and a "1.60-pip" criterion 5 bar giving a margin of 0.47 ± 0.66. These are **superseded**. They predate the 2026-11-25 HistData DST fix, and the 1.60 substitution was withdrawn by the spec's own evaluation note. Everything below uses the current as-run figures: prefix net +2.301 (t +3.47, n 881, SE 0.664) and postfix +1.683 (t +3.34, n 947, SE 0.503).

## Spread charging: instant quotes per leg are right; yearly medians are not

### Q1: the paying leg's own last quote is the field-standard object

The microstructure literature defines quoted, effective and realised spreads from **the quote prevailing at the instant of execution**. Mancini, Ranaldo and Wrampelmeyer compute effective cost "by comparing transaction prices with the quotes prevailing at the time of execution". They average the quoted spread over one-second EBS data only to build a daily liquidity index, not to charge a particular fill ([Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer 2011/JF 2013](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)). Karnaukh, Ranaldo and Söderlind use the same definitions on "prevailing bid and ask" quotes at one-second resolution ([Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind 2013/RFS 2015](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)). The closest published analogue to spec 004 is Krohn, Mueller and Whelan's Tokyo-fix strategy, long USD into 9:55 and short after. It charges each leg its own timestamped quote: Δs^pre = −s^a₁ + s^b₂, so the trade "makes money if bid prices at the fix are larger than the average of ask prices" at the session ends ([Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, BoC SWP 2021-48 / JF 2024](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)). The study's bid-anchored rule is algebraically identical. A long buys at bid + s at entry and sells at bid at exit, so its P&L is bid₂ − ask₁; the short postfix leg mirrors this at its exit. The 09:55 bar, where both legs sell, correctly pays no spread. **Evidence strength: field-standard.** The study agrees.

The literature's live caveat concerns **which feed** the quote comes from, not how it is sampled. Krohn et al. treat quoted indicative spreads as "considerably larger than the effective spreads", which makes their net returns "too conservative". Following Cespa et al., as reported by Krohn et al., they show results from zero to the full spread ([Krohn et al.](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)). On EBS for 2007–2009, USDJPY's quoted spread averaged 1.50 bp against an effective half-spread of 0.42 bp, so the effective full spread was about 56% of the quoted one ([MRW Table I](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)). Last look pushes the other way: a quote that can be rejected after the order arrives understates the cost of execution actually achievable ([GFXC](https://www.globalfxc.org/docs/gfxc_report_last_look.pdf)). The sobering precedent is that Krohn et al.'s yen fix pattern earns 12.0% a year gross but turns mostly negative at the full indicative spread, and earns 2.73% at half the spread ([Krohn et al.](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)). No source licenses charging a retail or IBKR-routed taker less than the observed quoted spread, so the study's refusal to stress downward is defensible.

The implied change is small and reported-only. Confirm that the "last quote" of the bar `quote_at` selects (ts − 1 min) is the quote in force at the fill instant. As a diagnostic at each of the three fill bars, also report how far the instant spread departs from the minute-mean spread. Ito and Yamada document "extreme concentration of trades" and price spikes at 9:55, and that is where the two could diverge ([Ito & Yamada, NBER w22820](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22820)).

### Q4: per-trade quotes beat aggregates, and aggregates are means

The hierarchy is the same in FX and in equities. **High-frequency observed spreads matched to the trade take precedence over any aggregate or low-frequency estimate wherever they exist.** Chen and Velikov "use high-frequency HF data to compute spreads whenever it is available". They found low-frequency spreads "biased upward by 25-50 bps after 2003", and they aggregate only because anomaly trades are hypothetical closing-auction trades with no matched quote ([Chen & Velikov, FEDS 2020-039 / JFQA 2023](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)). In FX, Karnaukh et al. show that a daily snapshot of indicative spreads tracks the EBS benchmark poorly, being "noisy and unrepresentative" partly because of when it is taken. They present low-frequency measures as a substitute for when HF data are unavailable ([KRS 2013](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)). When papers do aggregate, they use **means**. Chen and Velikov take share-weighted daily averages and then simple monthly averages. Ito and Hashimoto average hourly EBS spreads over a year, separately for standard and daylight time ([Ito & Hashimoto, NBER w12413](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12413)). A median appears in this literature only as a robustness diagnostic. **Evidence strength: field-standard for "observed per trade over aggregate"; my inference that a median understates expected cost.** Spreads are floored and spike upward, so the median sits below the mean. The direction is certain, and the size has to be measured.

Three kinds of conditioning matter, and a pooled yearly median flattens all of them. The first is **hour**. Ito and Hashimoto find the 09:00–10:00 JST hour wider than other Tokyo business hours even though deal counts peak then. The second is **calendar day**. Ito and Yamada show that 5/10 days and month-end change fix-time pricing, and Lee, Mucklow and Ready find spreads conditional on information events ([Lee, Mucklow & Ready 1993](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v6y1993i2p345-74.html)). The sign of a Gotobi-day spread effect is unknown: one search-summary report has spreads narrower in the WM/R fix minute ([Panagiotou](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/619c8b75e1ed4ba691e8ad6a006855e6/39-panagiotou---the-wmr-fix-and-its-impact-on-currency-markets-.pdf)). The third is **within-year time variation**: AUD/USD spreads rose 20-fold at the October 2008 peak ([MRW](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)). No source measures USDJPY spreads on Gotobi versus non-Gotobi days. This study's stage 2 sample, with one B and one C day per month, is the only way to learn it.

The study's rule disagrees on the median and partly agrees on pooling. Pooling B and C days (about 24 per year per fill bar) buys precision. A B-only yearly figure from 12 days would be noisy, and the declared rule keeps B-versus-C as a descriptive report, which is the right place for it. **Amendment (before filled data is viewed):** charge the yearly **mean** of the stage 2 sample at each fill bar, or keep the median and add the measured mean−median gap. In addition, wherever a trade's own Dukascopy quote exists (the stage 1 2023 fills), charge that quote rather than the yearly figure, as Chen and Velikov's "whenever available" rule requires. **Reported:** a KRS-style overlap validation on 2007–2014, where every weekday is fetched. For each year and fill bar, compare the "B+C sample-day median" estimator against the mean of actual per-trade spreads, so the stage 2 fallback's bias is known rather than assumed. Also report B-only against C-only spreads at the fill bars. A treatment-only spread increase comes straight out of the B net, so this is a hypothesis-relevant result, not decoration.

## Rebuilding costs across sources: measured first, impute the rest, key eras on liquidity

### Q2: substituting the spread is sound; dropping missing-quote trades is not

The anomaly-cost literature runs a strict hierarchy. Use the measured cost per observation where it exists, use a proxy validated on the overlap where it does not, and **never drop an observation because its cost is missing**. Novy-Marx and Velikov put it directly: "we cannot know at the time of portfolio formation if the trading cost estimate for any given stock will be missing when we want to unwind the position. We thus cannot limit our trading to stocks for which the direct estimates of trading costs are available." They impute by nearest neighbour, report that imputation covers 29% of observations but under 4% of market cap, and validate by re-imputing observations that do have direct estimates, finding differences of "less than one basis point per month" ([Novy-Marx & Velikov 2015 WP / RFS 2016](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)). Chen and Velikov fill their ISSM and TAQ holes the same way, with calibrated low-frequency proxies framed as a "backcast of the unobserved high frequency effective spread" ([Chen & Velikov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)). **Evidence strength: field-standard for the hierarchy and the no-drop rule. No published staleness tolerance exists for minute-bar FX.** The Holden–Jacobsen quote delays cited by Chen and Velikov concern timestamp precision, not staleness, so the 5-minute window is a declared choice, not a standard one.

The first half of the study's rule holds up better than one research note suggests. That note worried that "bid_A + spread_B" mixes two venues' midpoints. Because the export bid prices both legs, though, P&L is bid_A(t₂) − bid_A(t₁) − s_B(t₁): **no cross-source level offset enters the trade**, and Δnet equals −Δspread exactly. That is the clean decomposition of a data-source effect and a cost effect which the field's practice implies. For 2007–2014 the export is itself third-party Dukascopy, so the substitution is effectively same-venue. For 2015–2024 the measured spread is a Dukascopy-venue cost applied to HistData-venue prices, which is acceptable, but it is a proxy for IBKR either way.

The second half disagrees with the standard. Criterion 5 governs on the measured column, so excluding no-quote trades turns the governing test into a complete-case analysis. Missingness here is not random: the repo's known holes cluster by hour and era. **Amendment (before filled data is viewed):** a trade with no Dukascopy quote within 5 minutes is charged the mean measured spread for its (fill bar × year) cell, flagged as imputed, and kept in the governing column. The complete-case version (the current rule) is reported beside it with the count of trades it drops. If the count turns out negligible (well under 1% of trades), the two columns will agree and the amendment costs nothing. **Reported:** the measured column under a 1-minute tolerance, as a sensitivity check on the declared 5.

### Q3: uniform assumption columns beside a best-available governing column is the field pattern

Published papers use **best-available cost per observation for the headline**, with uniform columns as bounds. Chen and Velikov splice ISSM, Monthly TAQ, Daily TAQ and low-frequency proxies, filling dated holes such as NASDAQ's missing April–May 1987 with proxies ([Chen & Velikov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)). Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling and Schrimpf run one uniform conservative assumption, "the full quoted bid–ask spread", which they call a lower bound on profitability, beside gross returns ([Menkhoff et al. JFE 2012](https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf)). Neely and Weller use fixed per-decade costs before their data begin and measured costs, scaled, afterwards ([Ivanova, Neely & Weller appendix](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/frbsl_wp/2014-033_appendix.pdf)). The quoted-to-effective scale factor is **contested**: Menkhoff et al. use 1×, while Neely and Weller divide by 3 on traders' say-so. Sources are not interchangeable either. Daily EBS and Reuters spreads correlate only 0.04–0.28, and Reuters spread volatility for USDJPY is more than twice EBS's ([KRS fn. 13](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)). **Evidence strength: field-standard.**

The study's design matches this. Filled trades get the Dukascopy bid and the synthetic profile in the as-run and era columns, which keeps those columns comparable across filled and unfilled trades. Real spreads appear only in the governing measured column. What the splicing papers add is a duty to show how the seam behaves: neither Chen and Velikov nor Neely and Weller present a hybrid without saying so. **Reported diagnostics:** a per-trade source flag in the measured column (Dukascopy own quote, stage 2 yearly figure, imputed, Eightcap), with the share of trades and of P&L from each. Add a missing-at-random check comparing filled and as-run trades' gross mid returns, and the ratio of measured to synthetic spread by year and fill bar, which is Chen and Velikov's level-bias plot. The Eightcap seam at 2024-06-19 changes venue. The only pre-holdout overlap is stage 2's H2-2024 sample days against Eightcap, about six days per arm. Use that, and do not extend the comparison into 2025 holdout data.

### Q6: date-keyed multipliers are the field's last resort, not its default

Practice runs from calendar-step costs, which Neely and Weller use only where nothing is measured, through measured costs keyed by source, to a **measured liquidity proxy calibrated on the overlap**. In FX the best proxy is volatility, with an average correlation of 0.81 with EBS effective cost. A composite of Corwin–Schultz, Gibbs and volatility reaches 0.93, and Karnaukh et al. use it to backcast to 1991 ([KRS](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)). Calendar multipliers miss short stress spikes: effective spreads tripled around Black Wednesday and quadrupled after Lehman. The documented USDJPY structural breaks do not sit at 2015. EBS moved from pip to decimal pricing in 2010–11, when USDJPY's quoted spread was at its one-pip floor much of the time ([Mahmoodzadeh & Gençay](https://www.fmg.ac.uk/sites/default/files/images/Tick%20Size.pdf)). A 2012 half-pip reversal is reported for USDJPY by [LeapRate](https://www.leaprate.com/news/icap-ebs-goes-to-half-pip-pricing) but not by Mahmoodzadeh and Gençay. Spreads kept narrowing into 2016 while price impact for larger orders rose after 2013 ([Hasbrouck & Levich 2019](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/jhasbrou/Research/Hasbrouck-Levich%20v25.pdf)). The study's 2015 step is a data-source boundary, not a liquidity boundary. **Evidence strength: field-standard that calibrated proxies beat calendar steps. The break chronology is sourced; its consequence for 2015 is my inference.**

The anchors put the era assumption at the conservative end. For 2007–2012, EBS USDJPY averaged 1.501 bp quoted and 0.401 bp effective per side ([KRS Table 1](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)). At a mean price near 95 that is roughly 1.4 pips quoted and 0.76 pips effective round trip (my arithmetic). The ×5 multiplier gives 1.80 pips at the 08:00 JST entry and 1.36 at the 11:00 exit. That is at or above the all-hours EBS quoted average and about twice the EBS effective cost. Whether a retail or ECN taker paid nearer the quoted or the effective figure is unsourced. The study's choice is **acceptable as a stress column and would be wrong as a governing one**, and the spec already makes measured govern. **Reported:** once stage 3 lands, estimate the multipliers rather than assert them. For each year and fill bar, give the measured mean spread divided by the synthetic profile for 2007–2014 (complete) and 2015–2024 (stage 2 sample), and show the estimates beside ×5 and ×2.5. Optionally add a volatility-keyed era column. Leave the declared era columns as they are. A useful integrity flag (my inference): Dukascopy spreads well below one pip before March 2011 would sit inside EBS's tick floor and deserve scrutiny.

## Validation and missing data: declare the fallback before the fetch ends

### Q5: step 0 is in line with the field; add fine lags and scale the threshold

No source sets an absolute cross-source tolerance in pips or bp. The field-standard cleaning rules **scale their thresholds to the data**. The quote rules from Barndorff-Nielsen, Hansen, Lunde and Shephard, as implemented in R `highfrequency`, delete spreads above 50× the day's median ([rmLargeSpread](https://rdocumentation.org/packages/highfrequency/versions/0.6.5/topics/rmLargeSpread)) and mid-quotes more than 10 MADs from a rolling median ([rmOutliersQuotes](https://rdrr.io/cran/highfrequency/man/rmOutliersQuotes.html); [BNHLS 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ect:emjrnl:v:12:y:2009:i:3:p:c1-c32)). These thresholds are defended by how little they remove, not by theory. Brownlees and Gallo find that dirty versus clean matters more than which cleaning algorithm is used ([Brownlees & Gallo 2006](https://ideas.repec.org/p/fir/econom/wp2006_03.html)). Olsen's FX filter is deliberately strict right after a data gap ([US patent 7742959](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7742959)). Cross-venue FX validation compares dynamics as well as levels. Danielsson and Payne find indicative quotes lagging firm ones by 2–3 minutes, with spread behaviour differing persistently, and they describe those differences rather than gating on them ([Danielsson & Payne 2002](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/119126)). Hoffmann, Rosenbaum and Yoshida give the standard lead-lag estimator, which identifies fine shifts ([HRY 2013](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1303.4871)). Arnott, Harvey and Markowitz require integrity checks "as a first step" and transformations "decided in advance" ([Arnott, Harvey & Markowitz 2019](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3275654)). **Evidence strength: field-standard on ex ante declaration and relative scaling. That a fixed 0.3-pip gate should be anchored to a stated scale, and that ±60-minute offsets cannot see minute-level lags, are my inferences.**

The study's 2023 design agrees with this. HistData is a different venue, so the comparison is reported without a gate, as Danielsson and Payne do. Running it against both the corrected and the uncorrected series is good documentation practice under Arnott et al. It also yields a free check: the uncorrected series should show the 60-minute discrepancy only between 12 and 24 March. The usual worry, that the comparison is circular if the DST correction was derived from Dukascopy, does not apply here. The spec records that the correction was found on existing HistData before anything under `data/tokyo_hours/` was read beyond filenames. **No gate change.** The declared 0.3-pip median applies to 2007–2014 and should stand; filled trades take both legs from Dukascopy, so a level offset cannot reach their P&L anyway. **Reported diagnostics:** state the gate against its scale. It is 0.83 of the 0.360-pip entry-hour spread and 13% of the 2.301-pip prefix effect per fill, or up to 26% per round trip if errors do not cancel. Report the full distribution of differences (median, mean, p90, p99, share above one spread), offsets of −2, −1, +1 and +2 minutes or a 1-minute return cross-correlation across shifts to catch bar-labelling errors, and the counts a BNHLS-style cleaning pass would flag on the new tree.

### Q8: waiting is legitimate, but an open-ended wait leaves the key decision undeclared

Economics, psychology and epidemiology converge on one rule: **neither abandon the plan nor silently proceed**. Run what is achievable, then report the planned analysis beside the achieved one and document every deviation's type, reason and timing. J-PAL asks researchers to "document why you made the edit" and to revise plans only at "designated milestones" ([J-PAL](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/resource/pre-analysis-plans)). Banerjee, Duflo and co-authors propose a "populated PAP" recording which intentions "were or were not achievable in practice" ([Banerjee et al. 2020](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/InPraise.pdf)). Willroth and Atherton propose a standard deviations table recording type, reason and timing ([Willroth & Atherton 2024](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/ampps/25152459231213802/)), and Lakens treats unforeseen events and missing information as legitimate grounds for deviation, judged by their effect on severity ([Lakens 2024](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/when-and-how-to-deviate-from-a-preregistration/)). STROBE item 12c requires counts missing at each stage, the assumed mechanism (MCAR/MAR/MNAR), and a complete-case comparison ([STROBE 12c](https://resources.equator-network.org/guidelines/strobe/items/statistical-methods-missing-data.html)). In finance, Arnott et al. say the sample "should never change after the research begins", so any forced change is a disclosed deviation. **Evidence strength: field-standard across those fields, with no finance-specific tick-data standard. Rubin argues that allowing deviations weakens what pre-registration buys ([Rubin 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.12347v13)), so the degree of permissiveness is contested.**

The study's rule agrees on the stopping rule, which does not depend on results, and partly disagrees on the rest. `download_gap_fill.py` caps restarts at 200, so "the fetch gives up" is a foreseeable state. Some hours, such as 25 December and holidays, may never return data, so "0 requests left" needs a definition that can actually be met. "Report to the user" defers the run-or-wait decision to a moment when partial data exists on disk, which is exactly when a decision is easiest to condition on what has been seen. **Amendment (before filled data is viewed):** first, define completion. An hour that returns empty after N declared retries is "permanently unavailable" and counts as done. Second, declare a deadline. Third, declare a fallback rule: if a stage is incomplete at the deadline, run on what exists, provided each arm's missing share is below a declared figure, with a deviations-table entry. Escalate to the user only if that bar is breached. **Reported:** filled, complete-case and difference columns; missingness tabulated by arm × fill bar × year; realised volatility on missing versus present bars (vendor feeds drop in fast markets, an MNAR risk); and a consolidated deviations table. The spec's dated amendments already contain the material for that table: the DST fix, the weekday-matched test, the criterion 5 evaluation note and the withdrawn 1.60 substitution.

## The tradeability test: net t, break-even with an SE, and bp at each trade's own price

### Q7: the field tests the net mean; criterion 5 tests a point margin that is twice as demanding

The dominant standard in FX and equity anomaly work is to **compute net-of-cost returns and test their mean** with a Newey–West t, showing gross, costs and net side by side. Menkhoff et al. judge whether net returns stay "economically high and statistically significant" ([Menkhoff et al.](https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf)). Neely, Weller and Ulrich test "the null that the Net AR equals zero" ([NWU 2006/JFQA 2009](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf)). Krohn et al.'s tradeability verdict for the Tokyo-fix reversal is itself a significance test: "transaction cost adjusted returns are significantly negative" ([Krohn et al. WP](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2022/Paper/Session_III_1a_Krohn__foreign_Exchange_Fixings_Returns_Around_The_Clock_0712.pdf)). Novy-Marx and Velikov report how many anomalies keep net t above 2 and, far fewer, above 3 ([Novy-Marx & Velikov](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)). Break-even cost is a standard **supplement**, and it comes in two forms. One is the cost at which significance is lost; the other is the cost at which the mean reaches zero. Momentum's 1.5% and 1.77% round-trip figures are the classic pairing ([Korajczyk & Sadka 2004](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/korajczy/htm/korajczyk%20sadka.jf2004.pdf)). Several cost scenarios are standard, but in FX they usually run **downward** from the quoted spread: Menkhoff et al. use 100/75/50%, each with its own t. **No source uses "net after base costs must exceed a second, pessimistic copy of all-in costs" as a gate**, and none names an explicit "not resolved" band. Academic FX reports returns and costs in bp at each observation's own price, and **no source re-prices a historical effect to today's price level**. **Evidence strength: field-standard for net t, break-even and bp. The cost-doubling gate and re-pricing are not attested. Reading the margin with a CI is my inference from standard confidence-interval logic.**

Three disagreements follow. The first is about labels. The spec's reported "not resolved within 1 SE" band corresponds to roughly 84% one-sided confidence, so the prefix margin at 1.28 SE is labelled "pass" even though its 95% lower bound is negative. Under any conventional 95% reading, **both as-run margins are not resolved**. The second is about the bar. Criterion 5 deducts base costs and then a full pessimistic set, so it requires the gross to cover about 2.4 times the base all-in cost, which no published test asks. The field's equivalent statement, break-even ÷ base cost, is 3.27× and 2.78× as run. The third is about headroom. On the field's own test, pessimistic net clears the repo's t = 2.5 bar with only **0.24 pips (prefix) and 0.03 pips (postfix)** of cost left over, and those figures use the 0.360 and 0.272-pip synthetic spreads. That is why the measured column decides everything.

The bp view at 159 is the weakest element. It multiplies the effect by 159/112 ≈ 1.42 on the untested assumption that the effect is constant in bp. Spread and slippage, which are quoted in pips, do not scale with it, so the view flatters the margin, and it flips the era prefix verdict from −0.815 to +0.52. Neely, Weller and Ulrich, and Chen and Velikov, state costs at each era's own level ([NWU](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf); [Chen & Velikov](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)). The study's own break-even in bp at each trade's price is the field-consistent number.

None of these changes alters criterion 5, whose threshold is pre-declared. **Reported diagnostics:** add a 95% label (one-sided LCB above zero means pass, UCB below zero means fail, otherwise not resolved) beside the existing 1-SE label. Report the insignificance break-even at t = 2.5 and at 1.645, the break-even ÷ base-cost multiple, and the net t in every cost column, including era, era ×2 and measured. Report a per-trade bp mean and t at each trade's own entry price. Relabel the 159 view as a forward projection, admissible only after a bp-stability test: split by price-level tercile or era, or regress per-trade pips on price, preferably also in EWMA-vol units as the repo's standardisation rules prefer. Keep live expectations on a separate line from the test. The 73% median backtest-to-live haircut ([Suhonen et al. via Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=36892)) takes the current annualised Sharpe of about 0.82 (2.301/19.70 per trade × √(881/18 years), my arithmetic) to about 0.22. Harvey and Liu's non-linear haircut falls hardest on marginal Sharpe ratios like this one ([Harvey & Liu 2015](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P120_Backtesting.PDF)).

## Conclusion

The standards shift where spec 004's risk sits. The fill mechanics, charging the instant quote on the paying leg with the bid anchored on both legs, are the literature's own convention and need no defence. The real exposure is in three aggregation choices that each tilt toward a pass: a median instead of a mean, a complete-case governing column, and a 1-SE band that turns an interval straddling zero into "pass". Each is small alone. Together they act on an effect whose cost headroom under pessimistic assumptions is already measured in hundredths to tenths of a pip. A related caution comes from the Krohn–Mueller–Whelan precedent: the closest published version of this trade, on all days rather than Gotobi days, is profitable at about half the quoted spread and not at the full spread. The measured column is therefore likely to land in exactly the zone where these choices decide the label.

The practical consequence is that the remaining blind window is valuable and short. Three amendments must be dated before any filled file is opened, or they become post-hoc: mean-not-median with own spreads where they exist, imputation with flags instead of exclusion, and a completion definition with deadline and fallback for the fetch. Everything else can safely be a reported diagnostic: the 95% label, insignificance break-even, estimated era multipliers, fine-lag offsets, missingness by arm, and the deviations table. A field-literate reviewer would also expect the write-up to call the governing result what the standards would call it: a net-t pass under base and pessimistic costs, and an unresolved margin against the cost-doubling bar until the measured column says otherwise.
