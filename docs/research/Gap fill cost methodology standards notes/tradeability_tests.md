# Field-standard tests of tradeability after costs (FX first, equity anomalies where FX is silent)

Scope note. Evidence labels used throughout:
- **[FS]** field-standard: done the same way across several primary sources found here.
- **[CC]** common but contested: used, but primary sources disagree on it.
- **[INF]** my inference. It is not a claim made by any cited source.

Full text was extracted and read for: Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf (2012), Krohn, Mueller & Whelan (2022 working-paper version of the JF 2024 paper), Neely, Weller & Ulrich (2006 WP version of JFQA 2009), Harvey & Liu (2015), Novy-Marx & Velikov (2015 WP version of RFS 2016), Chen & Velikov (FEDS 2020-039 version of JFQA 2023), Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer (WP version of JF 2013), and Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind (2013 first draft of RFS 2015). ## Q7a. What is the standard test that an anomaly survives transaction costs: t on net returns, break-even cost, or a point-estimate margin over a cost bar?

### Takeaway
The dominant standard in both FX and equities is to compute **net-of-cost returns and test them for significance** (t-stat, usually Newey-West, on the net mean), and to report the gross figure, costs and net side by side **[FS]**. Break-even cost is a widely used **complementary** summary, mostly in momentum and technical-trading work, that converts the margin into a cost level **[FS as a supplement, not as the test]**. None of the sources found uses a bare point-estimate margin over a cost bar, with no standard error, as the decision rule.

### Cited Findings
- **FX, t on net (Menkhoff et al. 2012, JFE).** Currency-momentum tables report net excess returns after deducting bid-ask spreads, with "t-statistics based on Newey and West (1987) standard errors" in brackets. Tradeability is judged by whether the net returns stay "economically high and statistically significant". The headline gross spread is up to 10% p.a., and "momentum portfolios… are significantly skewed towards minor currencies that have relatively high transaction costs, accounting for roughly 50% of momentum returns". — [Menkhoff, Sarno, Schmeling & Schrimpf 2012, JFE 106(3), "Currency momentum strategies"](https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf); [BIS WP 366 version](https://www.bis.org/publ/work366.pdf)
- **FX technical rules, t on net (Neely, Weller & Ulrich 2009, JFQA).** Tables report Gross AR, then "Net AR adjusts for the variable transaction cost". The t-stat "uses Newey-West standard errors to test the null that the Net AR equals zero". They also report a net Sharpe ratio and trades per year. — [Neely, Weller & Ulrich, St. Louis Fed WP 2006-046B](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf); [JFQA 44(2):467-488](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jfinqa:v:44:y:2009:i:02:p:467-488_09)
- **FX intraday fix reversals, the closest analogue to this study (Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, JF 2024).** Gross reversal effects are about 2.5 bp per window, with t-stats of 4.5-11.7. For a liquidity demander, "reversal returns turn negative once transaction costs are taken into account… the unconditional return patterns cannot easily be arbitraged away", and "transaction cost adjusted returns are significantly negative". The tradeability verdict therefore rests on a significance test of the net return, here a significantly negative one. Costs come from indicative bank-to-client bid and ask quotes (Refinitiv Tick History). The authors note that RTH data cannot show "the price at which transactions are executed". — [Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, WP version (VSFX 2022)](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2022/Paper/Session_III_1a_Krohn__foreign_Exchange_Fixings_Returns_Around_The_Clock_0712.pdf); [JF 79(1):541-578](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:79:y:2024:i:1:p:541-578); [Bank of Canada SWP 2021-48](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- **Equity anomalies, t on net at two bars (Novy-Marx & Velikov 2016, RFS).** Each table reports the gross return, alpha, turnover, transaction costs, net return and net alpha. Costs "always reduce strategy profitability… increasing data-snooping concerns". "While many of the strategies' net excess returns remain significant at the t-statistic greater than two level, far fewer… generate net excess spreads with t-statistics greater than three." They also introduce a "generalized alpha" for evaluating net performance. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov, NBER w20721](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721); [author PDF](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf); [RFS 29(1):104-147](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v29y2016i1p104-147..html)
- **Equity anomalies, expected net return after costs, decay and data-mining shrinkage (Chen & Velikov 2023, JFQA).** The study covers 120 anomalies using effective bid-ask spreads. The FEDS version finds that the average anomaly earns about 8 bp/month net and the strongest 10-20 bp/month after data-mining corrections; the JFQA abstract gives about 4 bp/month on average and at best 10 bp for the strongest. The figures differ between versions, so cite the version used. The best ranking signal was the **in-sample net Sharpe ratio**. — [Chen & Velikov, FEDS 2020-039 abstract page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/zeroing-in-on-the-expected-returns-of-anomalies.htm); [FEDS PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf); [JFQA 58(3):968-1004](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-financial-and-quantitative-analysis/article/zeroing-in-on-the-expected-returns-of-anomalies/945133D5A3ECEEAF466AEE91551FD225)
- **Break-even cost in equity momentum (secondary attribution).** "At round-trip transactions costs of 1.5%, the profits on a long/short momentum strategy become statistically insignificant. At round-trip transactions costs of 1.77%… driven to zero." The search snippet came from Korajczyk & Sadka (2004, JF), and its attribution to Grundy & Martin (2001, RFS) could not be confirmed from text. **Note the pairing:** a cost at which *significance* is lost (1.5%) is reported next to a cost at which the *point estimate* hits zero (1.77%). — [Korajczyk & Sadka 2004, JF](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/korajczy/htm/korajczyk%20sadka.jf2004.pdf)
- **Lesmond, Schill & Zhou (2004, JFE).** The stocks that generate large momentum returns are those with high trading costs, so the large gross spreads create an "illusion of profit opportunity". Abstract and search snippet only. — [Lesmond, Schill & Zhou 2004, JFE](https://www.bauer.uh.edu/rsusmel/phd/Lesmond_et%20al%20_2004_JFE.pdf)
- **Capacity break-even (Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz).** Using $1.7tn of live executions, they report break-even *fund sizes* (US size/value/momentum: $103bn, $83bn and $52bn) and find costs "many times smaller" than the literature assumes. Short-term reversal "do[es] not survive trading costs at reasonable size". Search snippets only. — [Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz, "Trading Costs", SSRN 3229719](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3229719); [AQR WP](https://www.aqr.com/insights/research/working-paper/trading-costs-of-asset-pricing-anomalies)
- **FX technical-rule break-even costs.** Several FX papers report break-even one-way costs in bp. One search summary cites a range of 2-97 bp for short-term momentum and at least 58.5 bp per one-way trade for a value strategy. Not verified from full text, so treat as illustrative only. — [search result: Rubio 2004 WP](https://econwpa.ub.uni-muenchen.de/econ-wp/fin/papers/0405/0405033.pdf); [Zarrabi, Snaith & Coakley 2017](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/18805/1/1-s2.0-S1057521916301958-main.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INF]** The FX papers closest to this study (Krohn et al., Menkhoff et al., Neely et al.) all decide tradeability by the **significance of the net mean**, not by whether a net point estimate clears a cost bar. A point-estimate rule without an SE is outside field practice.
- **[INF]** Break-even cost is the natural way to say how much cost headroom exists. Its field use comes in **two versions**: the cost that makes the net mean zero, and the cost that makes it insignificant (the 1.77% vs 1.5% pairing above). The second is the conservative and more informative one.
- **[INF]** The standards are layered: (1) net t-stat under base costs; (2) the same under alternative cost scenarios; (3) break-even cost; (4) a multiple-testing or out-of-sample discount. Chen & Velikov is the fullest example, netting costs, post-publication decay and data-mining shrinkage in one expected-return number.

### Gaps
- I could not read Grundy & Martin (2001) directly to confirm the 1.5%/1.77% attribution. The figures came from a Korajczyk-Sadka search snippet.
- I could not read Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski & Rebelo (2011, RFS) in full. Snippets confirm only that they use interbank bid/ask quotes, that spreads rise with order size, and that spreads narrowed after 1999 ([NBER w12489](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12489/revisions/w12489.rev0.pdf)).
- The search found no source that names a point-estimate margin over a cost bar as an accepted standard, and none that names it as an error.

## Q7b. Is cost-doubling, or another stressed cost multiplier, a recognised robustness check? How do papers report base vs stressed costs?

### Takeaway
Reporting results under **several cost scenarios** is field-standard in FX **[FS]**. In FX, however, the variation usually runs **downward** from the quoted spread, which is treated as an *upper bound*: Menkhoff et al. use 100%/75%/50% of quoted, and Levich & Thomas give a "conservative" 4 bp against 2.5 bp. **Time-varying** era cost schedules are also standard (Neely et al.). I found **no primary source that uses "net after base costs must exceed a second, pessimistic copy of all-in costs" (literal cost-doubling) as a pass/fail rule.** The nearest standard equivalent is to state the break-even cost as a multiple of the base cost.

### Cited Findings
- **Scenario grid, with quoted spreads treated as too high (Menkhoff et al. 2012).** "bid–ask spreads from BBI/Reuters are based on indicative quotes that are too high (see, e.g., Lyons, 2001) relative to actual effective spreads… our results with net returns… should be understood as undercutting the lower bound on the profitability." Following Goyal & Saretto (2009), they report net returns at 100% (Table 7 Panel A), 75% and 50% (Panel B) of the quoted spread. Each scenario has its own Newey-West t-stat. Example, f=1, h=1: net 3.92% under the full quoted spread, 5.28% [t 2.98] at 75%, 6.64% [t 3.76] at 50%. — [Menkhoff et al. 2012, JFE](https://faculty.washington.edu/ss1110/IF/Sarno%20Currency%20Momentum%20JFE%20%281%29.pdf)
- **Base and "conservative" costs, plus an era-declining schedule (Neely, Weller & Ulrich).** They cite Levich & Thomas (1993), who derive 2.5 bp per futures transaction and "describe 4 basis points per transaction as more 'conservative'". Earlier work (Chang & Osler 1999; NWD 1997) used 5 bp per position change. NWU's own costs "decline from 10 basis points on January 1, 1973 to 1.88 basis points on June 30, 2005, for each change of position". They also note that "electronic trading has lowered transactions costs significantly". — [Neely, Weller & Ulrich WP 2006-046B](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf); companion cost series (one-way, about 5 bp in the 1970s falling to about 3 bp in the 1990s for majors) per the search summary of [St. Louis Fed WP 2014-033 appendix](https://fraser.stlouisfed.org/files/docs/publications/frbsl_wp/2014-033_appendix.pdf)
- **Effective vs indicative spreads in intraday FX (Krohn et al.).** They show the reversal patterns survive in firm quotes and in traded prices and "are thus not absorbed by the effective bid-ask spread". Their net-of-cost test still uses indicative bank-to-client quotes. — [Krohn et al. WP](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2022/Paper/Session_III_1a_Krohn__foreign_Exchange_Fixings_Returns_Around_The_Clock_0712.pdf)
- **Whether costs are too high or too low is itself contested [CC].** Chen & Velikov: "Spreads are a lower bound trading cost because they correspond to the smallest market orders". They reject Frazzini et al.'s argument that limit-order trading earns the spread, because of adverse selection and execution risk. Frazzini, Israel & Moskowitz: actual costs are "less than a tenth as large as" literature estimates (as quoted by Novy-Marx & Velikov). — [Chen & Velikov FEDS PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf); [Novy-Marx & Velikov](https://mysimon.rochester.edu/novy-marx/research/ToAatTC.pdf)
- **FX costs rise with order size, and small-ticket estimates understate them.** Burnside et al. find spreads rise with order size ([NBER w12489](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w12489/revisions/w12489.rev0.pdf), snippet). Melvin et al. argue that cost estimates built for small trades understate costs for larger ones ([CESifo WP 8143](https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp8143.pdf), snippet). An IMF WP measures effective client spreads *in pips* on EUR/USD forwards: 6.9 pips per transaction vs 18.1 pips at client level ([IMF WP 2019/100](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2019/wpiea2019100.pdf.md), snippet).
- **FX liquidity spikes in stress, so cost stress is state-dependent, not a constant multiplier.** Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind: the GBP/USD effective spread, around 0.5 bp in August 1992, "increased approximately by three times". After Lehman the AUD/USD effective spread rose "by 4 times (i.e. from 0.9 to 4.1 b.p.)". Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer give a USD/JPY bid-ask of 0.90 bp at its minimum pre-crisis level. — [Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind, 2013 draft](https://ecb.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf); [RFS 28(11) 2015](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:28:y:2015:i:11:p:3073-3108.); [Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer, WP version of JF 2013](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/141852/files/SSRN-id1447869%5B1%5D.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INF]** "Stressed costs" are recognised, but the **direction depends on the cost source**. With indicative quoted spreads, FX papers stress *down*, because quoted spreads exceed effective ones. With measured or modelled execution costs and possible era drift, as in this study's synthetic spreads, stressing *up* is appropriate, and an era-declining schedule (Neely et al.) is the published precedent for era multipliers.
- **[INF]** Read literally, the study's kill criterion requires net after base costs to be at least the pessimistic all-in cost. That is equivalent to **gross ≥ base all-in + pessimistic all-in**, i.e. break-even cost ≥ about 2× the cost level. It is stricter than any rule found in the literature, which is defensible as a tradeability bar. It differs from field practice in two ways: (a) it is applied to a point estimate with no SE; (b) it double-counts, deducting base costs and then a full pessimistic set rather than replacing base with pessimistic. The field-standard alternatives are: re-run the net test under pessimistic costs, and report the break-even cost with its CI as a multiple of base cost.
- **[INF]** Cost stress in FX is concentrated in identifiable states (crises, rollover hours), as the KRS and Mancini figures show. A uniform multiplier is therefore cruder than a state- or hour-keyed spread, and the repo's execution-hour spread fix is in line with that.

### Gaps
- I found no published FX or equity paper whose stated criterion is "net exceeds a second copy of costs" or "survives 2× costs". The 2× rule may be practitioner folklore; I could not source it.
- I did not obtain Burnside et al.'s exact treatment of quoted vs effective spreads.

## Q7c. How should uncertainty in the cost-adjusted margin be reported: confidence bounds, "not resolved" zones, haircuts?

### Takeaway
The field reports **standard errors and t-stats on the net mean, per cost scenario** **[FS]**. It then applies **multiple-testing and out-of-sample discounts** on top. Harvey & Liu's Sharpe haircut is non-linear and heavy for marginal Sharpe ratios. The published live and post-publication decay figures are large: a median 73% Sharpe decline backtest-to-live; 26% lower returns out-of-sample and 58% lower post-publication. An explicit "not resolved" zone for the *margin over costs* is not a named field convention **[INF]**, but it follows directly from testing net-of-stressed-cost returns.

### Cited Findings
- **Haircut Sharpe ratio (Harvey & Liu 2015, JPM).** Convert the Sharpe ratio to a t-ratio, adjust the p-value for multiple tests (Bonferroni/Holm/BHY), and convert back. The haircut is non-linear: "when the Sharpe ratio is greater than 1.0, the haircut is at most 25%… the 50% rule of thumb… is too lenient for relatively small Sharpe ratios (< 0.4) and too harsh for large ones (> 1.0)… Marginal strategies are heavily penalized because they are likely false discoveries." They note that "the 50% haircut is generally considered the industry standard" among practitioners. The framework also gives a **minimum profitability hurdle**: given significance level, number of observations, volatility and number of tests, "the minimum average monthly return that the proposed strategy must exceed". They use 316 documented factors (HLZ) as the test count and correlation model, and say a true out-of-sample test is "a cleaner way to evaluate a strategy's viability" than a historical holdout. — [Harvey & Liu 2015, "Backtesting", JPM Fall 2015](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P120_Backtesting.PDF); [CFA digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2016/03/backtesting-digest-summary)
- **Example magnitude.** A practitioner write-up applies Harvey's formula to turn a 0.75 Sharpe ratio into 0.32, about a 60% haircut (secondary source). — [Advisor Perspectives 2016](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/articles/2016/04/28/special-edition-can-you-trust-investment-research)
- **Backtest-to-live haircut (Suhonen, Lennkh & Perez 2017, JPM).** Across 215 alternative-beta strategy indices from bank sponsors, the median backtest Sharpe was 1.20 against 0.31 live, a **median 73% decline**. After fixed effects, the most complex strategies suffer haircuts more than 30% larger than simpler ones. Secondary summaries only (the full text was paywalled, and the fetch returned 403). — [Alpha Architect summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/?p=36892); [Risk.net](https://risk.net/node/5297666)
- **Post-publication decay (McLean & Pontiff 2016, JF).** Across 97 predictors, portfolio returns are "26 percentage points (pps) lower" out-of-sample before publication and "58 pps lower after publication". The authors reject that predictability disappears entirely. — [CFA Institute digest](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/2016/06/does-academic-research-destroy-stock-return-predictability-digest-summary); [JF 71(1):5-32](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:71:y:2016:i:1:p:5-32)
- **Combined expected net return (Chen & Velikov).** Costs, post-publication decay and empirical-Bayes or out-of-sample data-mining shrinkage combine into a single expected net return. Most anomalies' net in-sample performance "can be largely explained by the null of no predictability", and right-tail returns are "mostly due to luck". — [Chen & Velikov FEDS PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)
- **Net-of-cost decay in FX.** Neely, Weller & Ulrich find FX technical-rule excess returns from the 1970s-80s "had disappeared by the early 1990s for filter and moving average rules". Fitted declines average 21 bp per year for the Sweeney rules and up to 71 bp per year for a JPY/USD channel rule. A currency-anomaly study using real-time data finds profits "decrease substantially after the publication" (search summary). — [Neely, Weller & Ulrich WP](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf); [City research repository JFQA paper](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/id/eprint/21718/1/fxregimeRevision-20190210-JFQA-FINAL.pdf)
- **Deflated Sharpe ratio.** Bailey & López de Prado (2014, JPM) correct the Sharpe ratio for the number of trials, non-normality (skew and kurtosis) and track length. Cited from prior knowledge and not fetched this session. Harvey & Liu describe López de Prado's related work: "only seven trials are needed to obtain a spurious two-year backtest that has an in-sample realized Sharpe ratio of more than 1.0". — [Bailey & López de Prado, SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2460551); [Harvey & Liu 2015](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P120_Backtesting.PDF)

### Inferences
- **[INF]** The margin over a cost bar is an estimate with an SE: net mean minus bar, divided by SE(net mean), since the bar is a known constant. A margin whose 95% CI straddles zero is in a "not resolved" zone. Margin significant above zero means pass; margin significantly below zero means fail, as in Krohn et al.'s significantly negative net.
- **[INF]** Break-even cost can be given a CI the same way. Gross mean ± 1.96·SE maps one-for-one into a range of break-even per-trade costs, because cost is a per-trade constant. Field papers report the point break-even and sometimes the "insignificance" break-even (the Grundy-Martin style 1.5% vs 1.77% pairing). Reporting both is the closest published analogue to a resolution zone.
- **[INF]** The haircut literature is about *expected live performance*. It belongs in a separate "expected live net" line, not in the significance test itself. The repo already applies the 73% figure in its two-bars table; Harvey & Liu imply that the haircut for a Sharpe ratio around 0.74 is larger than for strong strategies.

### Gaps
- I found no source that formally names a "not resolved" or indeterminate zone for cost-adjusted margins. The concept is an inference from standard CI logic.
- I could not verify Suhonen et al.'s methodology from full text.
- I found no FX-specific backtest-to-live haircut estimate. The 73% comes from multi-asset bank alternative-beta indices.

## Q7d. Is it standard to express returns and costs in bp rather than pips, and to re-price historical effects at today's price level?

### Takeaway
Academic FX work expresses both **returns and costs in basis points or percent of price** **[FS]**. This holds for intraday returns (Krohn et al.), spreads and effective costs (Mancini et al.; Karnaukh et al.), and trading-rule costs (Neely et al.). Pips appear mainly in microstructure and client-cost descriptions (IMF). I found **no source that re-prices a historical effect to today's price level**. The standard is to convert each observation to a return at **its own** price, and to model cost changes over time explicitly (era-declining schedules), not by rescaling.

### Cited Findings
- **Intraday FX returns in bp.** The dollar portfolio "depreciates by around 2.5 basis points in the run up to the Tokyo and European fixes"; "Table IV lists the daily returns in basis points"; annualised returns are given as percentages. — [Krohn et al. WP](https://www.wu.ac.at/fileadmin/wu/d/ri/isk/VSFX_2022/Paper/Session_III_1a_Krohn__foreign_Exchange_Fixings_Returns_Around_The_Clock_0712.pdf)
- **FX spreads and effective costs in bp.** USD/JPY bid-ask "0.90bps" at its pre-crisis minimum; one-minute returns reduced by "0.013 to 0.172 basis points" per order-flow unit. — [Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer WP](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/record/141852/files/SSRN-id1447869%5B1%5D.pdf). GBP/USD effective spread "around 0.5 basis points"; AUD/USD "from 0.9 to 4.1 b.p." — [Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind 2013 draft](https://ecb.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)
- **Trading-rule costs in bp per position change, time-varying.** Costs declining from 10 bp (1973) to 1.88 bp (2005). — [Neely, Weller & Ulrich WP](https://files.stlouisfed.org/files/htdocs/wp/2006/2006-046.pdf)
- **Pips used for client effective spreads.** EUR/USD forwards: 6.9 pips at transaction level vs 18.1 pips at client level (search snippet). — [IMF WP 2019/100](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2019/wpiea2019100.pdf.md)
- **Equity anomaly costs also in proportional units.** Effective spreads and net returns are expressed in bp/% per month. Chen & Velikov note that costs vary strongly by era: they "plummet in the 2000's as electronic trading and decimalization have improved liquidity". — [Chen & Velikov FEDS PDF](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INF]** A pip is a fixed price increment (0.01 JPY), so its bp value is 1/price × 100. It is about 0.89 bp at 112 and 0.63 bp at 159. Within 2007-2024 USDJPY traded from the mid-70s to about 160 (approximate, from general knowledge), so a fixed pip effect varies about 2× in return terms across the sample. Averaging pips across that range mixes economically different quantities. The field-consistent approach is to convert **each trade** to bp at its own entry price, then average.
- **[INF]** Re-pricing the sample-mean effect from about 112 to 159 assumes the effect is **constant in bp**, and then states it in today's pips: ×159/112 = ×1.42. That assumption is untested and convenient. It inflates the pip effect by 42% while spread and slippage, which are quoted in pips, do not scale. In pips, the commission is the only cost that scales with price. A re-priced comparison therefore flatters the margin unless the bp-stability of the effect is shown first. Ways to show it: split by price-level tercile or era and test the bp effect for equality; or regress per-trade pips on price.
- **[INF]** The cleanest price-invariant alternative is **vol-units**: the effect divided by contemporaneous EWMA vol. This matches the repo's own standardisation rules and the scaling that inventory or flow mechanisms would plausibly follow. bp is the field default for reporting; vol-scaling is the better invariance test.
- **[INF]** Costs should be expressed at **each era's own level**, as Neely et al. and Chen & Velikov do, not at today's level. The "today" view is a separate forward-looking feasibility estimate: today's measured costs in bp against the effect's bp estimate, *if* bp-stable. Label it as a projection, not as a re-statement of the backtest.

### Gaps
- I found no published guidance that explicitly endorses or rejects re-pricing a historical pip effect to the current price level. The absence itself suggests it is not standard practice.
- I found no source comparing whether short-horizon flow effects in USDJPY scale with price level (bp-constant) or with volatility.

## Implication for this study (one paragraph per sub-question)

- **7a.** Spec 004's net t (+3.15 prefix, +3.11 postfix, after base costs) is the field-standard test, and it passes at the t=3 level that Novy-Marx & Velikov use as the stricter bar. The kill criterion's literal form is a point estimate over a cost bar with no SE, which is not field practice.
