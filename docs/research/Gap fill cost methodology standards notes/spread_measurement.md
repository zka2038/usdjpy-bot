# Spread measurement for simulated fills: field-standard practice (FX first, equity where FX is silent)

Scope: (Q1) which spread a simulated fill at a fixed clock time should be charged (the quote at the fill instant, a quote averaged over an interval, or an effective spread), and (Q4) whether per-trade observed spreads or a sampled or aggregate summary is the standard, including seasonality and event-day versus control-day spreads.

How the sources were read: the full text of five PDFs was extracted and read directly: Mancini, Ranaldo and Wrampelmeyer (MRW) 2011 working paper; Karnaukh, Ranaldo and Söderlind (KRS) 2013 ECB workshop version; Krohn, Mueller and Whelan (KMW) Bank of Canada SWP 2021-48; Ito and Yamada NBER w22820; Ito and Hashimoto NBER w12413; Chen and Velikov FEDS 2020-039. Other items rest on abstracts or search summaries, and each one says so where it is cited. No dukascopy.com URL was fetched.

Labels used below: **[STANDARD]** means field standard, **[CONTESTED]** means common but contested, **[INFERENCE]** means my own reasoning.

## Q1: Which spread to charge a fill at a given time (the quote at the fill instant, an interval average, or an effective spread)

### Takeaway
The microstructure literature defines all three measures from the quote **prevailing at the instant of the trade**. The "quoted spread" in liquidity studies is that instantaneous ask minus bid, averaged across the day only so that it can serve as a daily liquidity index. The FX backtest closest to this study (KMW 2024 JF, trading around the 9:55 Tokyo fix) charges **the bid or ask at each leg's own timestamp**: ask at entry, bid at exit, so that spread variation across time is kept. The effective spread is the measure that matches real execution cost, and it is usually smaller than the quoted spread. FX backtests on indicative quote feeds treat the full quoted spread as a conservative upper bound and report results across a range of fractions of it. Nobody standard charges a minute-average spread in place of the prevailing quote.

### Cited Findings

**Definitions (quoted, effective, realized)**
- [STANDARD] MRW define the proportional quoted bid-ask spread as L(ba) = (P^A − P^B)/P^M, with P^M = (P^A + P^B)/2. They then state: "In practice trades are not always executed at the posted bid or ask quotes. Instead, deals frequently transact at better prices. Effective costs can be computed by comparing transaction prices with the quotes prevailing at the time of execution." Effective cost is (P − P^M)/P^M for buyer-initiated trades and (P^M − P)/P^M for seller-initiated trades. It is therefore a **half-spread** measure. — [Mancini, Ranaldo & Wrampelmeyer, "Liquidity in the Foreign Exchange Market: Measurement, Commonality, and Risk Premiums", working paper Dec 2011, §III.B; published J. Finance 68(5), 2013](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)
- MRW footnote 12 explains why effective cost can be lower than half the quoted spread ("new traders may come in, executing orders at a better price… traders may post hidden limit orders which are not reflected in quoted spreads") and why it can be higher ("the spread may widen if the size of an order is particularly large"). — [MRW 2011](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)
- MRW's daily aggregation: "Daily estimates of illiquidity are obtained by averaging the effective cost of all trades that occurred on day t". The quoted spread is "the average proportional bid-ask spread computed using intraday data for each trading day", from EBS data "on a one-second basis". — [MRW 2011, §III.A–B and Table I notes](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)
- **USD/JPY magnitudes on EBS, 2007–2009** (MRW Table I, means of the daily measures, USD/JPY column): quoted spread **1.50 bp** (SD 0.41), effective cost **0.42 bp** (SD 0.10), volume-weighted effective cost **0.27 bp**. EUR/USD is 1.05 / 0.31 / 0.21 bp. The effective cost is a half spread, so the implied effective full spread for USD/JPY is about 0.84 bp, roughly 56% of the average quoted spread. (I read the column-to-pair mapping from the table's header order in the extracted text.) — [MRW 2011, Table I](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)
- KRS use the same definitions: effective cost is computed "by comparing transaction prices with the quotes prevailing at the time of execution", and the quoted spread is BA = (P^A − P^B)/P. "Following the previous literature, we refer to the EC as the main benchmark measure". Their EBS data run Jan 2007 to May 2012 "on a one-second basis (i.e. 86,400 observations per day)… we use the prevailing bid and ask (offer) quotes". — [Karnaukh, Ranaldo & Söderlind, "Understanding FX Liquidity", ECB 3rd FX workshop version Sep 2013, §2–3; published RFS 28(11), 2015, 3073–3108](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)
- [STANDARD, equity regulatory] SEC Rule 600/605 defines the effective spread as 2 × |execution price − midpoint| and the quoted spread as best offer minus best bid **at the time of order receipt**. The realized spread is the effective spread measured against a **later** midpoint (the amended rule uses horizons from 50 ms to 5 min), so it captures temporary versus permanent price impact. — [Schwab Rule 605 explainer](https://schwab.com/legal/sec-605); [eCFR 17 CFR 242.600](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-242/subject-group-ECFRac68bdd026a46db/section-242.600); [eCFR 242.605](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-17/chapter-II/part-242/subject-group-ECFRac68bdd026a46db/section-242.605). These details come from search summaries of these pages, not from a full read.
- [STANDARD, equity anomaly backtests] Chen & Velikov charge "the effective half bid-ask spread—that is, the absolute difference between the trade price and the prevailing quoted midpoint… a buy trade 'overpays' by the effective half spread and a sell trade receives too little by the same amount. Effective spreads use trades that are actually executed, and typically imply smaller spreads than quoted prices due to price improvement (Stoll 2003)." Portfolio payoffs are reduced "by half of the effective bid-ask spread whenever a portfolio weight is adjusted." — [Chen & Velikov, "Zeroing in on the Expected Returns of Anomalies", FEDS 2020-039, §2.2; JFQA 58(3) 2023, 968–1004](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)
- Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) calculate anomaly transaction costs "using the effective bid-ask spread measure proposed by Hasbrouck (2009)", which is a low-frequency estimate from daily data. — [Novy-Marx & Velikov, "A Taxonomy of Anomalies and their Trading Costs", RFS 29(1) 2016, 104–147; NBER w20721](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721). This rests on the abstract and search summary.

**What the closest FX backtest actually charges (Tokyo fix)**
- KMW test strategies that go long USD before the 9:55 Tokyo fix and short after it, using Refinitiv Tick History indicative bid and ask quotes. They charge the **instant-specific** quote on each leg: "The legs of the trade take into account inter-temporal variation in spreads: Δs^pre_t = −s^a_1 + s^b_2, Δs^post_t = +s^b_2 − s^a_3, where superscript a (b) refer to the ask (bid) price", so the strategy "makes money if bid prices at the fix are larger than the average of ask prices at the beginning and end of the trading session." This is the same convention this study uses: buy at the ask, sell at the bid, each at its own timestamp. — [Krohn, Mueller & Whelan, "Foreign Exchange Fixings and Returns Around the Clock", BoC SWP 2021-48, §V Transaction Costs; published J. Finance 79(1) 2024, 541–578](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- KMW treat the quoted spread as a **proxy for**, and an overstatement of, the effective spread: "Using the quoted high-frequency bid and ask prices from our benchmark RTH data set as a proxy for the effective spread… there is evidence that these spreads are considerably larger than the effective spreads based on firm quotes and executed trades, leading to measured net returns that are too conservative". They cite Gilmore & Hayashi (2011). — [KMW 2021, §V](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- KMW also report: "Cespa, Gargano, Riddiough, and Sarno (2021) compare the bid-ask spreads from Datastream with quoted prices from other data providers… and they suggest decreasing indicative spreads by up to 75% in order to obtain a more realistic proxy of the transaction costs that big traders in the over-the-counter FX market face." KMW therefore "take an agnostic approach and report results for different spread levels ranging from zero… to the full reported bid-ask spreads". This is a secondhand report of Cespa et al.; I did not read the RFS 2022 article itself. — [KMW 2021, §V](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf); [Cespa et al., "Foreign Exchange Volume", RFS 35(5) 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v35y2022i5p2386-2427..html)
- KMW result for the yen around the Tokyo fix: 12.0% annualised gross. "Incorporating the full transaction costs adjustment as implied by the indicative quotes… reverses the picture, and average returns for most windows turn negative". At 50% of the spread the yen earns 2.73%. Their Tokyo windows are long USD 5:00 p.m.–8:55 p.m. New York time and short USD 8:55 p.m.–2:00 a.m. — [KMW 2021, §V, Table IX](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- KMW also find the fix pattern in Refinitiv Matching traded prices ("and are thus not absorbed by the effective bid-ask spread") and in CME/ICE firm futures quotes. — [KMW 2021, §III robustness](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)

**Firm versus indicative quotes; last look**
- [STANDARD] EBS quotes are firm: "All EBS quotes are transactable, in other words, they reliably represent the prevalent spot exchange rate" (MRW §III.A). EBS covers "the vast majority of spot trading" in EUR/USD and USD/JPY (MRW, citing Chaboud, Chernenko & Wright 2007). — [MRW 2011](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585). Chaboud et al. (2004) likewise contrast live, transactable EBS quotes with the indicative quotes used in earlier work. — [Fed IFDP 823](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2004/823/ifdp823.htm) (search summary).
- KRS find that a daily bid-ask spread built from **single daily snapshots of indicative quotes** (Datastream, snapped at 21:00 GMT) tracks the EBS benchmark poorly. "The indicative quotes probably affect also the BA. In addition, the timing (21.00 GMT) of the daily bid-ask spread seems to lead to a noisy and unrepresentative measure of the transaction costs." — [KRS 2013, §4/5](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)
- KMW describe RTH quotes as an aggregator of individual bank quotes "available to market participants to trade 'bank-to-client'", from which "we cannot gauge… the price at which transactions are executed". — [KMW 2021, §II data](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)
- Last look: the liquidity provider supplies a quote rather than a firm price and keeps the option to reject an order after receiving it. The FX Global Code's Principle 17 was tightened in Dec 2017, and the GFXC later said no additional hold times should be applied. — [NBIM, "The role of last look in foreign exchange markets", 2015](https://www.nbim.no/en/publications/asset-manager-perspectives/2015/the-role-of-last-look-in-foreign-exchange-markets); [GFXC last look report](https://www.globalfxc.org/docs/gfxc_report_last_look.pdf); [Oomen, "Last look", Quantitative Finance 17(7) 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/quantf/v17y2017i7p1057-1070.html). These rest on search summaries; the papers were not read in full.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] Every definition above is anchored to the **prevailing quote at the trade time**. A fixed-clock simulated fill that takes the last ask and bid of the 1-minute bar ending at the trade time is therefore the quoted-spread analogue of the field-standard object, provided the bar's last quote is the quote in force at the fill instant (CLAUDE.md trap 6 covers the ts−1min alignment). A minute-average spread is a smoothed index, not the cost of a specific fill. It differs from the instant value precisely when spreads jump within the minute, for example at a fix or at the rollover.
- [INFERENCE] MRW's one-second averages are effectively **time-weighted** quoted spreads, because each second gets equal weight. They exist as daily liquidity indices, not as charges for a specific fill. Using a time-weighted average for a fill is defensible only when the exact fill time is unknown, which is not the case here.
- [STANDARD per KMW, CONTESTED in magnitude] Charging the full quoted spread from a non-firm or aggregator feed is the **conservative** convention. Effective cost on firm interdealer quotes is roughly half the quoted spread (MRW USD/JPY: 0.84 bp effective full spread against 1.50 bp quoted). Whether retail or ECN fills land nearer the quoted or the effective spread is not settled. Last look cuts the other way: a quoted price can be rejected when the market moves, so a feed with last look can **understate** the cost of execution that is actually achievable at the quote.
- [INFERENCE] Dukascopy (an ECN/retail aggregator) is neither EBS firm interdealer nor a Datastream daily snap. Its spreads are probably wider than EBS and tighter than indicative bank-to-client quotes, but I found no primary source that measures this. A study that is not executing as an interdealer should not apply MRW's effective/quoted ratio of about 0.56 to Dukascopy quotes.

### Implication for this study
Charging each leg its own prevailing quote, with the ask on the buy leg and the bid on the sell leg at the exact fill instant, is the field-standard convention. It matches KMW's Tokyo-fix backtest one-for-one, so the bar-last-quote method is correct in kind. The literature's main caveat is about **which feed** is used, not how the quote is sampled. A quoted spread from a non-interdealer feed is an upper bound on effective cost for a large trader and possibly a lower bound for a small trader facing last look. The defensible reporting is therefore KMW-style: net results at the observed quoted spread, plus a sensitivity band (for example 0.5× to 1.5× the observed spread, or an era-scaled spread). Results should not be presented as hinging on one spread level. The study's own pessimistic cost should stay at or above the full observed quoted spread, because no source licenses charging less than the quoted spread to a retail or IBKR-routed taker.

### Gaps
- No primary source benchmarks **Dukascopy** quotes against EBS/Refinitiv Matching or against realised retail or IBKR fills. Forum posts exist only on dukascopy.com, which was off-limits. This is the binding unknown for this study.
- I did not read Cespa, Gargano, Riddiough & Sarno (RFS 2022) directly. The "reduce indicative spreads by up to 75%" figure is KMW's report of their finding.
- I did not obtain López de Prado (AFML), Chan, or Kissell's specific guidance on which spread to charge, so I cannot state what those texts say without inventing it.
- Evans & Lyons, Neely & Weller, Menkhoff–Sarno–Schmeling–Schrimpf: not checked in this pass. From prior knowledge only (unverified here), the currency-portfolio literature typically uses daily or month-end Datastream bid/ask snaps, the kind KRS show to be noisy.
- The MRW numbers are from the 2011 working-paper Table I. The published JF 2013 table may differ slightly.

## Q4: Per-trade observed spreads versus a sampled or aggregate summary; seasonality; event-day versus control-day; median versus mean

### Takeaway
The hierarchy is consistent across FX and equity: **observed high-frequency spreads matched to the trade beat any aggregate or low-frequency estimate whenever they exist**. Aggregates are used only when a trade's exact time and quote cannot be matched, as with monthly anomaly portfolios at the closing auction. Low-frequency or snapshot summaries are documented to be biased or noisy against high-frequency benchmarks. FX spreads have strong, well-documented intraday seasonality: wide in the 18–22 GMT dead zone, wide in the first Tokyo hour relative to the rest of Tokyo, and wide over the Tokyo lunch. Spreads also move with trading activity and information events, so a pool of calendar days can misstate the cost on event days. Daily and monthly aggregates in this literature are **means**; I found no standard that uses a median as the cost charge.

### Cited Findings

**High-frequency observed versus low-frequency or sampled summaries**
- [STANDARD] Chen & Velikov: "low-frequency spreads are upward biased by 25-50 bps after 2003… We account for this bias by using high-frequency spreads from NYSE's Trade and Quote (TAQ) database. Spreads from TAQ serve as benchmark measures of liquidity in the microstructure literature (Goyenko, Holden, and Trzcinka 2009; Fong, Holden, and Trzcinka 2017), and indeed all low frequency (LF) spreads demonstrate their validity by examining their correlations with HF spreads". "We use high-frequency HF data to compute spreads whenever it is available." Their conclusion: "High-frequency data is necessary as low-frequency spreads are biased upward in recent years." — [Chen & Velikov FEDS 2020-039, §1, §2.2, §5](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)
- They say the Novy-Marx & Velikov (2016) low-frequency costs "overestimate expected costs going forward" (Figure 2 plots the median LF−HF difference over time). — [Chen & Velikov 2020](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)
- When they do aggregate, they use **means**: "we first aggregate to a daily level by taking a share-weighted average of intra-day spreads, and then aggregate across days within each month by taking a simple average following Hanna and Ready (2005)". They aggregate only because the anomaly trades are hypothetical monthly closing-auction trades: "An alternative method for measuring trading costs is to exclusively use intraday data as in Knez and Ready (1996), but this would deviate significantly from the anomalies literature which is based on closing auction prices." — [Chen & Velikov 2020, §2.2](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2020039pap.pdf)
- [STANDARD, FX] KRS validate low-frequency FX liquidity proxies against EBS high-frequency benchmarks by time-series correlation. The best low-frequency composite reaches 0.93 correlation with EBS effective cost, and the indicative-quote daily snapshot spread performs poorly. They present low-frequency measures as useful when high-frequency data are **unavailable**, citing high-frequency data's "very limited access only to recent data". — [KRS 2013, §1–2, §4](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/events/pdf/conferences/131216/Third_FX_workshop_RANALDO_UnderstandingFXLiquidity_KRS_20Sep2013_FULL.pdf)
- KMW, the closest analogue, use per-trade, per-timestamp quotes rather than a period average (see Q1). — [KMW 2021, §V](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/swp2021-48.pdf)

**Time variation (constant versus time-varying costs)**
- MRW: FX spreads vary hugely over time. AUD/USD went from 2.64 bp to 54.03 bp at the October 2008 peak, "a 20-fold increase… not an isolated event during the crisis." Liquidity co-moves strongly with the VIX and TED spread (adj. R² 76% for market-wide FX liquidity). — [MRW 2011, §IV–V](http://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2012/retrieve.php?pdfid=585)
- KRS: FX liquidity "declines with funding constraints and global risk", with stronger co-movement in distressed markets. — [KRS 2013/RFS 2015](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2329738)

**Intraday seasonality (USD/JPY, EBS)**
- Ito & Hashimoto (EBS one-minute firm quotes for USD/JPY and EUR/USD, 1999–2001) compute the hourly bid-ask spread "for each GMT Hour (Hour 0 to 23), and then averaged over a certain period (mostly over a year)… with a differentiation of the standard and daylight saving time." So their seasonality profile is a **mean by hour, DST-aware**. — [Ito & Hashimoto, "Intra-Day Seasonality in Activities of the Foreign Exchange Markets: Evidence from the Electronic Broking System", NBER w12413; J. Japanese & Int'l Economies 2006](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12413)
- Findings: "quote revision frequency, trading volume, and return volatility are found to move together whereas the spread moves the opposite way, most of the time". "The peak in the globally non-active hours, 18 to 22 GMT hours, have the unambiguously high bid-ask spreads." "Hour 0 [GMT = 09:00–10:00 JST], when the bid-ask spread is higher than other business hours in Tokyo (except lunch hour at Hour 3) but the number of deals… is at one of the peaks; that is, unlike other times, a positive correlation." The Tokyo lunch-hour dip in activity persists after the 1994 deregulation. — [Ito & Hashimoto, NBER w12413, §1, §3.4](https://www.nber.org/papers/w12413)
- KMW anchor their day at 5:00 p.m. New York because spreads are widest and volume lowest then (as reported in a search summary of the paper). — [KMW, Bank of Canada](https://www.bankofcanada.ca/?p=223052)

**Fix windows and calendar or event days**
- Ito & Yamada (EBS tick data, Tokyo fix): "at the moment of the Tokyo fixing (or 9:55am), there is an extreme concentration of trades, generating frequent price spikes". "The price spike is even stranger since it occurs when liquidity is ample." Their fixing-rate regressions include "calendar dummies… dated on the 5th and 10th days, Friday, and the last trading day of each month", and "the calendar effects also matter for determination of the fixing rate and the price fluctuation around fixing time." They note that EBS one-second data can show negative spreads when counterparties lack credit lines. — [Ito & Yamada, "Puzzles in the Forex Tokyo 'Fixing'", NBER w22820; J. Int'l Economics 109 (2017) 214–234](https://www.nber.org/papers/w22820)
- A Norges Bank-hosted study of the WM/Reuters 4pm fix reportedly finds that bid-ask spreads were much **narrower** than usual during the one-minute fix window while volatility was higher. This is from a search summary only and was not read. — [Panagiotou, "The WMR fix and its impact on currency markets"](https://www.norges-bank.no/contentassets/619c8b75e1ed4ba691e8ad6a006855e6/39-panagiotou---the-wmr-fix-and-its-impact-on-currency-markets-.pdf)
- [STANDARD, equity] Spreads are conditional on information events. Lee, Mucklow & Ready (1993) find that spreads widen and depths fall before earnings announcements and that spreads are wider afterwards; the widening fades quickly once volume is controlled for. — [Lee, Mucklow & Ready, RFS 6(2) 1993, 345–374](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v6y1993i2p345-74.html) (abstract)
- In FX, macro announcements are followed by higher volume and volatility on EBS (Chaboud et al. 2004; Chaboud, Chernenko & Wright 2007). The abstracts I saw do not report spread effects. — [Fed IFDP 823](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/ifdp/2004/823/ifdp823.htm); [Fed IFDP 903](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/ifdp/trading-activity-and-exchange-rates-in-high-frequency-ebs-data.htm)

**Median versus mean**
- Every aggregate cost found in this pass is a **mean**: MRW (daily average effective cost; Table I means), Chen & Velikov (share-weighted daily average, then simple monthly average), Ito & Hashimoto (hourly spread averaged over a year). — sources as above. A median appears only as a **diagnostic**: Chen & Velikov's Figure 2 plots the median LF−HF difference, and KRS run median quantile regressions for robustness.

### Inferences
- [INFERENCE] For an expected-P&L calculation, the cost to subtract is E[spread at the fill instant on the trade's days]. Bid-ask spreads are bounded below and spike upward, so they are right-skewed, and a **median understates the mean cost**. A yearly median would make net returns look better than the mean-cost expectation. The direction is certain; the size depends on the tail and should be measured on the Dukascopy periods that are available.
- [INFERENCE] A yearly median from a calendar sample of one treatment day and one control day per month mixes in three pieces of conditioning the literature shows matter. (a) **Hour**: Ito & Hashimoto show the 09:00–10:00 JST hour is wider than other Tokyo hours. A sample is valid only if it is taken at the same clock minutes as the fills, with DST keyed the way CLAUDE.md trap 7 requires. (b) **Event versus control**: Ito & Yamada show 5/10 days and month-end change fix-time pricing. Lee–Mucklow–Ready and KMW imply that liquidity is conditional on order-flow events. So treatment-day spreads at 09:55 may differ from control-day spreads in either direction: more liquidity at the fix (Ito & Yamada's "ample" liquidity, Panagiotou's narrower fix spreads) or wider before it. A pooled median cannot show which. (c) **Time variation within the year**: MRW show 20-fold crisis swings, which a single yearly figure flattens.
- [INFERENCE] One defensible use of the sampled median is as a **fallback**, in the same way KRS and Chen & Velikov use low-frequency estimates, for periods where per-trade quotes do not exist. It should be validated, KRS-style, by comparing it against per-trade observed spreads in the overlap period. The validation should report the ratio of the mean per-trade spread to the sampled median, separately for treatment and control days, and then scale the fallback accordingly.

### Implication for this study
Where Dukascopy tick quotes exist for a trade's own minute, the field-standard choice is to charge **that trade's observed ask on the buy leg and bid on the sell leg**, as KMW and Chen & Velikov ("HF whenever available") do. Use the sampled yearly median only for periods with no quotes, and only after (i) checking it against the per-trade spreads in the overlap, by treatment versus control and by fill clock time, and (ii) converting it to a **mean**, or adding the mean−median gap, because a median understates expected cost under right skew. Report the event-versus-control spread difference at the fill minutes as a result in its own right. If treatment-day spreads at 08:00 or 09:55 JST differ from control-day spreads, a pooled cost misstates the B−C net effect directly, and in a hypothesis-relevant direction. A treatment-only cost increase would come straight out of the measured edge. Present the net result under at least the per-trade observed spread and a pessimistic scaled spread, KMW-style.

### Gaps
- No source found that measures **USD/JPY spreads on Gotobi days versus non-Gotobi days** at the 9:55 fix or at 08:00 JST. Ito & Yamada condition fixing *prices* on 5/10 days but, in the text I read, do not report spreads split by calendar day. This has to be measured in-sample.
- Ito & Hashimoto's hour-by-hour numerical values for GMT hour 23 (08:00 JST, the prefix entry) were not extracted. Only qualitative statements for 18–22 GMT, Hour 0 and Hour 3 were confirmed. Their data are 1999–2001 EBS, which predates today's market structure.
- I found no FX-specific paper that measures spreads around Japanese macro releases or BoJ events.
- No source compares per-trade spreads with a calendar-sample median directly. The median-versus-mean claim is my inference from right skew; it was not tested here.
- I did not verify the practitioner texts (López de Prado, Kissell) on time-varying versus constant cost models.
